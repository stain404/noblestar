Noble Star's FCL service gives you a safe, reliable and cost-efficient transportation
solution, tailored to your commodity. With a full container load, your cargo travels
alone — sealed at origin and opened at destination, with no consolidation warehouse and
no co-loader handling in between.

## Why shippers choose FCL

- **Fewer touchpoints.** The container is sealed once. Less handling means less damage
  and less pilferage risk.
- **Faster transit.** No waiting for a consolidation to fill, and no deconsolidation at
  the far end.
- **Better unit economics at volume.** Past roughly 13–15 cubic metres, FCL typically
  costs less per unit than LCL.
- **Equipment choice.** Reefers for temperature-controlled cargo, open-tops and flat
  racks for out-of-gauge, standard dry boxes for everything else.

## Equipment guide

| Container | Usable volume | Typical max payload |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 20ft standard | ~28 m³ | ~28 tonnes |
| 40ft standard | ~58 m³ | ~26 tonnes |
| 40ft high-cube | ~68 m³ | ~26 tonnes |
| 40ft reefer | ~59 m³ | ~25 tonnes |

Payload limits vary by carrier, road regulation and destination. We confirm the exact
figure for your booking rather than working from the table.

## Choosing equipment for the commodity

The table above answers "what fits". It does not answer "what should I book", and those
are different questions.

**Dense cargo hits weight before volume.** Tiles, stone, machinery, liquids in drums and
anything with metal in quantity will reach the payload ceiling with the container half
empty. For these, two 20ft containers frequently beat one 40ft, because the payload limit
is per box rather than per cubic metre. Shippers who assume a 40ft is always the better
deal routinely overpay here.

**Light cargo hits volume before weight.** Textiles, packaging, furniture and empty
containers of any kind will fill a 40ft high-cube long before troubling its payload
limit. The extra 30 centimetres of internal height on a high-cube is roughly ten more
cubic metres for the same footprint, which is usually the cheapest volume you will buy.

**Out-of-gauge needs a decision early.** Cargo that will not fit through the doors of a
standard box needs an open-top, a flat rack, or a breakbulk solution — and all three
need to be arranged well in advance, priced differently, and routed on services that
accept them.

**Temperature control is a commitment, not a setting.** A reefer runs at one setpoint for
the whole journey. Mixed-temperature consignments need parallel units, not compromise.

## Stuffing, sealing and VGM

Once the equipment is right, the loading matters. Weight should be distributed evenly
across the container floor rather than concentrated at one end, cargo should be braced so
it cannot shift at sea, and dunnage or airbags should fill voids that would otherwise let
a load walk. A container that arrives with collapsed pallets was usually loaded badly
rather than handled badly.

The seal number goes on the bill of lading and is checked at destination. A broken or
mismatched seal turns a routine clearance into an investigation.

**Verified gross mass** is the SOLAS requirement to declare the certified total weight of
the packed container before it can be loaded onto a vessel. There is no discretion in it:
no VGM, no loading. We file it as part of the booking, but the figure has to be accurate.
Carriers reweigh, and a container whose declared weight does not match the scale gets
rolled to the next sailing — which costs a week, not an afternoon.

## Free time, demurrage and detention

An FCL booking comes with a number of free days at each end. After those expire, two
different charges start:

- **Demurrage** is charged by the terminal while the container sits in the port.
- **Detention** is charged by the carrier once the container has left the port but has
  not been returned empty.

Free time varies by carrier, trade lane and contract, and it is one of the things worth
comparing between quotes rather than looking only at the freight rate. A cheaper rate with
three free days can easily cost more than a higher rate with ten, if your unloading
schedule is not immediate.

We flag the free time on the booking confirmation, and we would rather clear a
consignment early and have it sit in your warehouse than clear it late and have it sit in
the port.

## Shipper-owned versus carrier-owned containers

Almost all FCL moves on carrier-owned equipment: the carrier provides the box, you use
it, and you return it empty within the free time. It is the cheapest and simplest
arrangement, and it is what we book unless there is a reason not to.

Shipper-owned containers make sense in narrower cases — a one-way move into a location
where returning equipment is difficult or expensive, a commodity that will contaminate a
box and make it unacceptable for return, or a consignment that needs to sit loaded at
destination for weeks rather than days. Buying a box to avoid detention is occasionally
the cheaper arithmetic, and we will say so when it is.

## What we need to quote

Origin and destination, commodity, total weight, total volume or container count,
whether the cargo is stackable, and your target ready date. If it is temperature
controlled or hazardous, tell us at this stage — it changes the equipment and the
carrier.
