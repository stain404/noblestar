Sea freight remains the backbone of GCC trade, and it is where Noble Star does most of
its work. We move full containers and consolidated cargo through the region's major
gateways — Jebel Ali, Khalifa Port, Jeddah Islamic Port, King Abdulaziz Port in Dammam,
Hamad Port and Sohar — with contracted space on top-tier carriers and full visibility
from booking to delivery.

## What the service includes

Every sea freight booking is managed as a single file. That means one reference number,
one coordinator, and one invoice covering as much or as little of the chain as you need:

- **Origin services** — collection from your supplier, export customs clearance,
  container stuffing and terminal handling
- **Ocean leg** — carrier booking, bill of lading issuance, and cargo insurance if you
  want it arranged
- **Destination services** — import customs clearance, port charges, and delivery to the
  consignee's door anywhere in the GCC

## Cargo we handle

General and palletised cargo, project and out-of-gauge shipments, temperature-controlled
reefer cargo, liquid bulk in flexitanks or ISO tanks, and food consignments requiring
municipality inspection. Hazardous cargo is accepted subject to class, UN number and
carrier approval — tell us at the quoting stage so we book the right equipment.

## Choosing between FCL and LCL

As a rough guide, once your consignment passes roughly 13–15 cubic metres, a 20ft
container usually becomes cheaper than paying LCL rates per cubic metre — and it removes
the consolidation and deconsolidation steps, which shortens transit. Below that, LCL is
almost always the better answer. We will quote both if it is close.

## The gateways we work through

Jebel Ali is the region's largest container port and our home terminal. It has the
deepest carrier coverage of any GCC port, which matters for two practical reasons:
direct sailings are available on more trade lanes, so your cargo avoids a transhipment
leg, and when a sailing is rolled there is usually another within days rather than weeks.

Khalifa Port in Abu Dhabi serves the western emirates and the industrial zones around
it. Jeddah Islamic Port on the Red Sea is the natural gateway for western Saudi Arabia
and cargo arriving from Europe and the Mediterranean, while King Abdulaziz Port in
Dammam serves the Eastern Province and connects by road to Riyadh. Hamad Port handles
Qatar's container traffic on short-sea services from Jebel Ali. Sohar and Salalah in
Oman are significant transhipment hubs in their own right, and Salalah's position on the
main east–west trade lane means some Far East cargo reaches Oman faster than it reaches
Dubai.

Which gateway is right for a consignment is a question of the final delivery address,
not of habit. Cargo destined for Riyadh can arrive at Jebel Ali and move overland, or
arrive at Dammam and travel a much shorter distance by road. We price the realistic
options rather than defaulting to the port we use most.

## Incoterms, and who is actually paying for what

More disputes arise from a misunderstood incoterm than from anything that happens at
sea. The term you agree with your supplier decides where their responsibility ends and
yours begins, and it decides which costs are already inside the price you were quoted.

- **EXW (Ex Works)** puts everything on you from your supplier's door, including export
  clearance in their country. It looks like the cheapest purchase price and frequently
  is not.
- **FOB (Free On Board)** means your supplier delivers to the origin port and handles
  export clearance. You control the ocean leg and everything after it. For most
  importers buying regularly, this is the term that gives the best visibility of real
  landed cost.
- **CIF (Cost, Insurance and Freight)** means your supplier books the ocean leg. It is
  convenient, but you inherit their choice of carrier and routing, and destination
  charges are often higher than they would have been under your own booking.
- **DAP / DDP** put delivery at your door on the supplier. DDP additionally puts import
  duty and clearance on them, which in practice they price defensively.

If you are unsure which term you are buying on, send us the commercial invoice and we
will tell you what it commits you to before the cargo moves.

## What actually delays a sea shipment

Weather and port congestion get the blame, but in our experience the common causes are
duller and more preventable:

1. **Documentation that does not agree with itself.** The invoice, the packing list and
   the bill of lading must show the same values, weights and descriptions. A discrepancy
   is the single most reliable way to trigger an inspection.
2. **A rolled booking.** On busy lanes carriers overbook. Cargo booked late, or released
   late from the shipper, is the first to be rolled to the next sailing.
3. **VGM not filed or filed wrongly.** No verified gross mass, no loading. It is a
   mandatory SOLAS declaration and carriers do reweigh.
4. **Certificates that arrive after the cargo.** An original certificate of origin still
   in a courier envelope while the container sits on the quay accrues demurrage daily.

## Booking timeline

For a straightforward FCL export out of the UAE, we ask for the booking around seven to
ten days before the vessel cut-off. That allows time to secure equipment, position the
container for stuffing, file the export declaration and lodge the VGM without anything
being rushed. LCL needs slightly longer because the consolidation has to close. Urgent
bookings are possible and we take them regularly — but the earlier the file opens, the
more options remain open with it.

## Getting a quote

Send us the origin and destination, the commodity, the total weight and volume or
container count, your incoterm, and the date the cargo is ready. If any of that is still
uncertain, send what you have. We would rather ask two questions than return a number
built on a guess.
