To ease the hassle of paperwork, Noble Star handles customs clearance procedures
irrespective of the mode of transport — typically within 24 to 36 business hours of
receiving a complete file. Clearance is an in-house function, not something we
subcontract, which is why we can act quickly when something goes wrong.

## What the service includes

- **Declaration filing** for import, export, transit, re-export and free zone movements
- **HS code classification** and duty assessment, with a second review on high-value or
  ambiguous commodities
- **Permit and approval coordination** with the relevant ministries and municipalities
- **Inspection attendance** — we send someone to the inspection rather than leaving your
  cargo unrepresented
- **Duty and VAT payment** handled on your behalf where you would rather not tie up your
  own customs account

## Documentation we will ask for

Getting these right at the start is what keeps a clearance to 24–36 hours:

1. Commercial invoice showing the correct incoterm, currency and value
2. Packing list with gross and net weights matching the transport document
3. Bill of lading or air waybill
4. Certificate of origin, attested where the destination requires it
5. Any product-specific permit — food, cosmetics, electronics, telecom equipment and
   medical devices all have their own regimes

## The declaration types, and which one you need

Not every movement is an import. Choosing the wrong declaration type is expensive to
unwind, so it is worth knowing which is which.

**Import for home consumption** is the standard entry: goods enter the local market, duty
and VAT become payable, and the consignment is free to circulate.

**Free zone entry** moves goods into a designated free zone without duty becoming
payable. Duty is suspended, not waived — it falls due if and when the goods later enter
the mainland.

**Transit** moves goods across a territory without entering its market at all, under
customs control from entry point to exit point. This is what applies to a truck crossing
Saudi Arabia on its way to Qatar or Kuwait.

**Re-export** takes goods that entered a territory and sends them out again. Where duty
was paid on entry, a refund may be available, subject to the goods being identifiable and
the claim being made properly.

**Temporary admission** covers goods entering for a defined purpose and period —
exhibition stock, testing equipment, machinery for a specific project — against a
guarantee, on the basis that they leave again.

## HS codes and why they matter more than people expect

Every commodity crossing a border is classified under a Harmonised System code. That code
determines the duty rate, whether the goods are subject to a permit or standard, and how
likely the consignment is to be selected for inspection.

The GCC applies a common external tariff, with a standard rate on most goods and
exemptions or different treatment for specific categories. A misclassified consignment
therefore fails in one of two directions: you pay more duty than you owe, or you pay less
and create an exposure that surfaces later at audit, with penalties.

Classification is genuinely difficult for composite products, kits, and anything that
could reasonably sit under two headings. On high-value or ambiguous commodities we run a
second review rather than accepting the first plausible code, because the cost of being
wrong compounds over every future shipment of the same product.

## What happens when a consignment is selected for inspection

Selection is partly risk-based and partly random, so a clean history reduces the odds
without eliminating them. When it happens, the container is moved to an inspection area
and opened in the presence of a customs officer.

We attend. That is not a formality: an unrepresented inspection takes longer, questions
that could be answered on the spot instead become written queries, and cargo that has been
opened needs re-securing. Attendance also means we hear the reason for any finding
directly rather than reading it days later.

Consignments needing municipality or ministry approval — food, cosmetics, medical devices,
telecom equipment — follow a separate track and typically take two to four business days
rather than 24 to 36 hours. We tell you which track yours is on when the file opens, not
when it is late.

## Demurrage and detention are not the same thing

Both are charges for time, and shippers routinely conflate them.

**Demurrage** is charged by the terminal for a container occupying the port after its free
days expire. **Detention** is charged by the carrier for a container that has left the
port but has not been returned empty. A consignment stuck in clearance accrues demurrage;
a consignment delivered but not unpacked accrues detention.

Both are avoidable with an early file. This is the concrete reason we ask for
documentation before arrival rather than on arrival: free days are consumed by the
calendar, not by effort, and a certificate of origin still in transit while a container
sits on the quay costs real money every day.

## If your goods are already stuck

We take over files mid-stream regularly, and there is no awkwardness in it. Send us the
bill of lading and whatever documentation exists. We will tell you what is missing, what
the realistic release timeline looks like, and what the accrued demurrage exposure is —
before you commit to anything.
