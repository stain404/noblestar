Food is where Noble Star has built its deepest expertise. We operate packing facilities
with food-safe handling procedures and manage the customs clearance regime that food
consignments attract across the GCC — a regime that is materially stricter than for
general cargo, and where most costly mistakes are made before the goods ever ship.

## What the service includes

- **Temperature-controlled transport** — chilled, frozen and ambient, by reefer container
  and reefer truck
- **Packing and repacking** at our own facility, under food-safe conditions
- **Municipality registration** of products and labels
- **Health certificate and halal documentation** coordination
- **Customs clearance and inspection attendance**
- **Liquid bulk** in flexitanks and ISO tanks

## The three things that stop food consignments

In our experience, food cargo is delayed or refused for three reasons, in this order:

1. **Label non-compliance.** Missing Arabic text, missing importer details, or a
   production and expiry date format the inspector will not accept.
2. **Insufficient remaining shelf life.** Goods that left the factory too late arrive too
   close to expiry and are refused entry.
3. **Missing or mismatched health certificates.** The certificate must match the
   consignment exactly — same product, same batch, same quantity.

All three are avoidable, and all three are cheaper to fix before dispatch than after
arrival. If you are importing a food product into the GCC for the first time, send us the
label artwork and the specification before you place the order.

## Product and label registration, step by step

Registration is the part first-time importers underestimate, because it happens before
anything moves and it cannot be compressed once the cargo is on the water.

**1. Product registration.** The product itself is registered with the relevant
authority — in Dubai, through the municipality's Food Import and Re-export System. This
requires the full ingredient breakdown, the product specification, and details of the
manufacturer.

**2. Label approval.** The label artwork is submitted and assessed against the labelling
requirements. This is where most rejections happen, and the reasons are consistent:
Arabic text missing or incomplete, production and expiry dates in a format that is not
accepted, the importer's details absent, nutritional information not presented as
required, or claims on the pack that are not permitted.

**3. Consignment clearance.** Only once the product and label are approved can a
consignment be cleared against them. The registration is reusable for future shipments of
the same product; the per-shipment clearance is not.

The practical consequence is that registration belongs at the purchase order stage. A
supplier who prints a label without Arabic text has produced stock that cannot be
imported, and the choices at that point are re-labelling at destination, which we can do
at our facility, or returning the goods — both of which cost more than getting the
artwork checked first.

## Shelf life, and the rule that catches people

Across the GCC, a food consignment must arrive with a substantial proportion of its shelf
life still remaining. The commonly applied benchmark for short-life products is at least
half, with longer-life products treated proportionately. Requirements vary by market and
by product category, so treat this as the shape of the rule rather than a single number.

The trap is that shelf life is counted from production, not from dispatch. A product with
a twelve-month life that sat in the supplier's warehouse for four months before shipping
arrives with a meaningful part of its life already spent, and a long ocean transit can be
enough to push it under the threshold on arrival.

Confirm production dates with your supplier before dispatch, not after. We ask for them at
booking on food consignments for exactly this reason, and we would rather raise an
uncomfortable question in our office than have a container refused entry.

## Halal certification

Meat and poultry require halal certification from an approved certifying body in the
country of origin, and the certificate must correspond to the consignment in front of the
inspector — the same product, the same batch, the same quantities. Certificates issued by
bodies that are not recognised in the destination market are a recurring cause of
rejection, and it is worth confirming the certifier's standing before the order rather
than at the border.

Products containing animal derivatives that are not obviously meat — gelatine, certain
emulsifiers, some flavourings — attract the same scrutiny and surprise importers who had
not thought of their product as a meat product at all.

## Cold chain integrity

Reefer units are pre-cooled before stuffing, setpoints are confirmed in writing on the
booking, and we can supply temperature logs for the transit on request. For consignments
where cold chain evidence matters to your customer, ask for logging at the quoting stage
so the right equipment is booked.

Two practical points. First, a reefer maintains temperature; it does not pull down a warm
load. Cargo must be at temperature before it is stuffed, because a unit asked to chill a
full container from ambient will not achieve it in transit. Second, a container or trailer
runs at a single setpoint, so a consignment mixing frozen and chilled lines needs parallel
units rather than a compromise temperature that suits neither.

## Liquid bulk

Edible oils, juice concentrates and similar non-hazardous liquids move in flexitanks — a
single-use bladder fitted inside a standard 20ft container, typically carrying somewhere
around twenty thousand litres depending on the product's density and the flexitank
specification. It is a considerably cheaper way to move volume than drums or IBCs, and it
eliminates the packaging waste at the far end.

Where the product, the temperature requirement or the volume rules out a flexitank, ISO
tanks are the alternative. Tell us the product, the volume and any temperature or food-grade
lining requirement and we will specify which is appropriate.
