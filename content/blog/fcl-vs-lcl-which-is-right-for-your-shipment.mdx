The FCL-versus-LCL question has a rule of thumb attached to it, and the rule of thumb is
roughly right — but it hides enough exceptions to be worth understanding properly.

## The short answer

Below about **13 to 15 cubic metres**, less than container load consolidation is normally
cheaper. Above it, a 20ft full container usually costs less *and* moves faster. The exact
crossover moves with the lane, the season and the current rate environment, which is why
we price both when a shipment lands near the boundary.

## How each is priced

**LCL is priced on revenue tonne** — whichever is greater, your cargo's volume in cubic
metres or its weight in metric tonnes. Three cubic metres weighing one tonne is charged
as three. One cubic metre weighing three tonnes is *also* charged as three. Shippers of
dense goods — tiles, machinery parts, canned products — are frequently surprised by this.

**FCL is priced per container**, regardless of how full it is. A half-empty 20ft costs the
same as a full one. That is what makes it uneconomic at low volumes and increasingly
attractive as you fill the box.

## The costs that do not show up in the rate

This is where the simple comparison breaks down.

**LCL carries more per-shipment charges.** Consolidation and deconsolidation fees,
handling at both ends, and often a higher destination charge per revenue tonne than you
would expect from the ocean rate alone. On short lanes these fixed costs can be a large
share of the total.

**LCL is slower.** Your cargo waits for the consolidation to fill at origin and is broken
down at destination. That is typically three to five days added at each end. On a
Jebel Ali to Dammam move, LCL runs seven to ten days against four to six for FCL.

**LCL means more handling.** Your goods are loaded, unloaded and moved alongside other
shippers' cargo several times. Damage and pilferage risk is genuinely higher, and it
places more demand on your packaging.

**FCL ties up more working capital.** You are shipping a larger quantity at once, which
means more inventory sitting in transit and more cash committed up front.

## A worked example

Say you are moving 11 cubic metres of packaged goods weighing 4 tonnes from Jebel Ali to
Dammam. Charged on volume, that is 11 revenue tonnes of LCL. Depending on the rate of the
day, this often lands close to the cost of a 20ft container — which would also arrive
three to four days sooner and be handled far less.

At 11 cubic metres, in other words, the answer is genuinely not obvious. At 5 it is LCL.
At 20 it is FCL. In the middle, ask for both.

## What we need to price it

Origin and destination, total volume in cubic metres, total gross weight, whether the
cargo is stackable, and your required delivery date. If it is temperature-controlled or
