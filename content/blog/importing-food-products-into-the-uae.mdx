Food is the most heavily regulated cargo we handle, and the category where the most money
is lost to avoidable mistakes. Almost all of those mistakes are made before the goods
ship. Here is what to get right, in the order it matters.

## 1. Register the product and its label first

Before a food product can be imported into the UAE, the product and its label must be
registered with the relevant municipality — in Dubai, through the Food Import and
Re-export System.

The label must carry, in Arabic:

- Product name and full ingredient list
- Production and expiry dates
- Country of origin
- Net content
- Importer name and details
- Storage conditions

Label rejections are common and they are specific: a date format that does not match the
required convention, an ingredient named inconsistently with the specification, a missing
importer line. Send the artwork for review before the print run, not after.

## 2. Check remaining shelf life before dispatch

The GCC applies shelf-life rules at the point of import. The general expectation is that
a consignment arrives with a substantial proportion of its shelf life remaining —
commonly at least half for short-life products.

This has a practical consequence people miss: a product with a six-month shelf life that
spends six weeks in production and consolidation and another three at sea can arrive
outside the window. Work backwards from the arrival date, not forwards from the
production date.

Goods that arrive too close to expiry are refused entry. There is no appeal that gets
them cleared.

## 3. Match the health certificate to the consignment exactly

Health certificates from the country of origin must correspond to what is actually in the
container — same product, same batch, same quantity. A certificate covering a different
batch number, or a quantity that does not reconcile with the packing list, will be
queried.

Meat and poultry additionally require valid halal certification from an approved
certifying body. "Approved" is doing real work in that sentence: certificates from bodies
not recognised by the UAE authorities are not accepted, however legitimate the
certification itself.

## 4. Get the cold chain right

For chilled and frozen goods, the reefer must be pre-cooled before stuffing and run at a
confirmed setpoint for the whole transit. Confirm the setpoint in writing on the booking —
"frozen" is not a temperature.

If your customer requires evidence of cold chain integrity, ask for temperature logging
at the quoting stage so the right unit is booked. Retro-fitting that requirement after
departure is not possible.

## 5. Allow for inspection

Food consignments are inspected more often than general cargo, and samples may be drawn
for laboratory testing. Build two to four business days into your plan for this.
Perishable cargo held for inspection is losing shelf life, which is why the first four
points matter so much — an inspection you pass cleanly costs days, one you fail can cost
the consignment.

## The short version

Register the product and label before you order. Check the arrival-date shelf life, not
the production date. Make the health certificate match the container exactly. Confirm the
reefer setpoint in writing. Expect an inspection.
